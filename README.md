# The Euclidean algorithm is not optimal among uniform processes

Euclid's algorithm computes gcd(a, b) with about 1.44 log₂ a remainder operations in the
worst case. Van den Dries and Moschovakis asked whether any algorithm restricted to the
remainder operation and a zero test can do essentially better. Moschovakis's *Main
Conjecture* says no: for every such algorithm α there is an r > 0 such that

$$\mathrm{calls}_{\mathrm{rem}}(\alpha, a, b) > r \log a \quad \text{for infinitely many pairs } a > b \ge 1.$$

Lower bounds like this are normally proved for Moschovakis's *uniform processes*, an
abstract model of any computation that learns about its inputs only through the given
operations; every program gives one. Moschovakis conjectured that the logarithmic lower
bound already holds there: Conjecture 3.4 of his 2012 notes, and the precise version
(4F-5) of the Main Conjecture in his 2019 book.

**Main result: those uniform-process conjectures are false.** Every pair a ≥ b ≥ 1 has a
derivation of gcd(a, b) with

$$\bigl(6 + o(1)\bigr) \frac{\log_2 a}{\log_2 \log_2 a}$$

remainder calls, where each call takes the remainder of two values already computed. So
the *intrinsic complexity*
of gcd and of coprimeness, which bounds the cost of every uniform process from below, is
o(log a). This refutes Conjecture 3.4 and (4F-5). With the book's Theorem 4F.1, a single
uniform process attains this cost, ruling out the possibility raised on p. 188 of the book
that the Main Conjecture holds for all uniform processes. It also means the homomorphism
method, the standard tool for such lower bounds, cannot prove a logarithmic one.

**Still open: the Main Conjecture for algorithms.** A derivation chooses which values to
combine for free, so it does not give a program with this cost, and no single program with
o(log a) remainder calls is known.

**Also proved.** The known Ω(log log a) lower bound, previously proved for specific hard
pairs, holds for almost all coprime pairs. For each ε > 0 there is a fixed
nondeterministic gcd program, with a fixed number of registers and no recursion, with cost
at most ε log₂ a + Bε; the program depends on ε.

**Verified.** Everything above is machine-checked in Lean 4, except the step that uses the
cited Theorem 4F.1; the refutations of Conjecture 3.4 and (4F-5) do not need it. The
result has not yet been reviewed by an expert in the area. The terms are defined under
[Notation](#notation).

**AI transparency.** This work was produced entirely by AI systems, including the main
result, the Lean formalisation and the manuscript; the human author initiated the
research and directed the AI systems. The paper ends with a full declaration.

## Results

Logarithmic remainder-call lower bounds for gcd and coprimeness cannot follow from
uniformity alone. Every pair a ≥ b has a remainder-only derivation of gcd(a, b) using
O(log a / log log a) remainder calls. Each call takes the remainder of two values
already available, starting from a and b; earlier values are kept and no numerical
constants are introduced. Euclid needs about 1.44 log₂ a calls in the worst case
(successive Fibonacci numbers).

### Notation

- G(a, b) is the least number of remainder calls in such a derivation of gcd(a, b).
  D(a, b), for coprime a, b, is the length of a shortest remainder derivation of 1; it
  equals G(a, b) there.
- Nε = (ℕ, rem, eq0, eq1) is the Euclidean structure, where eq0 and eq1 are the tests
  x = 0 and x = 1, and ⊥⊥ is the coprimeness relation (x, y ≥ 1 and gcd(x, y) = 1).
- A *certificate* for a relation or function f at inputs a, b is a finite partial
  substructure generated by a, b on which every homomorphism into the structure respects
  f at (a, b) (the Homomorphism Test). The *intrinsic calls(rem)-complexity*
  calls(rem)(Nε, f, a, b) is the least number of remainder facts in a certificate; a
  remainder fact corresponds to a remainder call.
- The ε in Nε is the book's name for the Euclidean structure, not the small ε > 0 in
  the bounds. The book also writes ε for the Euclidean algorithm; like the paper, we
  write Eucl.
- Bε is a constant depending only on ε.
- log means log₂; the base is irrelevant inside O and o.

### Upper bound and refutations

The refined upper bound is **(6 + o(1)) log₂ a / log₂ log₂ a**, uniformly in b.
Precisely, for every ε > 0, the coefficient 6 + ε works for all sufficiently large a
and every 0 ≤ b ≤ a. The same leading coefficient holds for the intrinsic
remainder-call complexities below on positive inputs. No optimality of 6 is claimed.

As a consequence, the intrinsic calls(rem)-complexity of both coprimeness and gcd in
Nε is o(log a), uniformly over pairs with 1 ≤ b ≤ a. This refutes the uniform-process
form of van den Dries and Moschovakis's conjecture that the Euclidean algorithm is
optimal among its peers:

- **Conjecture 3.4** of Moschovakis's 2012 notes, *Absolute lower bounds for uniform
  processes*. We prove there is no real r > 0 with calls(rem)(Nε, ⊥⊥, a, b) ≥ r log a
  for infinitely many pairs a ≥ b ≥ 1. The notes' exact wording (rational r, infinitely
  many values of a) is refuted too.
- **The "precise version" (4F-5) of the Main Conjecture** in Y. N. Moschovakis,
  *Abstract Recursion and Intrinsic Complexity*, Lecture Notes in Logic 48, Cambridge
  University Press / ASL, 2019, doi:10.1017/9781108234238 (p. 151; all page citations
  refer to this published edition). It asks for a set D ⊆ ℕ² and a K > 0 such that

  $$(x, y) \in D \implies \mathrm{calls}_{\mathrm{rem}}(\mathrm{Eucl}, x, y) \le L(x, y) \le K \cdot \mathrm{calls}_{\mathrm{rem}}(\mathcal{A}, \gcd, x, y),$$

  where L(x, y) = 2 log max(x, y), with L(0, 0) = 0, and Eucl is the Euclidean algorithm. We prove that for
  every K > 0 the second inequality holds on only finitely many pairs. This holds both
  for gcd certificates without eq1 facts (𝒜 = (ℕ, rem, eq0)) and for certificates in Nε.
  The book's form, with a set D on which the complexity is unbounded (condition (4F-4)),
  is refuted as well. These statements omit the first inequality, which only weakens the
  statement being refuted.
- **The possibility raised after Corollary 6C.7 (p. 188)**, that the Main Conjecture
  "holds for all uniform processes of Nε". Ruling this out also uses the book's
  best-uniform-process theorem (4F.1), which turns a small intrinsic complexity into a
  uniform process with that complexity. Theorem 4F.1 is cited, not formalised.

### Further results

- **Almost-all lower bound.** For every ε > 0, among coprime pairs b < a ≤ N, all but
  o(N²) have D(a, b) > (1 − ε) log₂ log₂ N. So the known Ω(log log a) lower bound,
  previously proved for specific hard pairs, holds for almost all coprime pairs.
- **Program family.** For every ε > 0 there is a fixed nondeterministic gcd program,
  using only rem and eq0, with a fixed number of registers and no recursion, whose cost
  is at most ε log₂ a + Bε on all positive inputs a ≥ b. Written one function per line, it
  is also a tail-recursive program in the book's sense. See
  [Program family](#program-family).
- **The homomorphism-test barrier.** The Homomorphism Test applied to all generated
  primitive operation diagrams cannot prove a logarithmic lower bound: every input has a
  certificate with o(log a) remainder facts. This leaves open arguments combining
  homomorphisms with additional restrictions on computations.
- **Closure depth.** The bound settles the Open Problem at the end of §3 of van den Dries
  and Moschovakis, *Arithmetic Complexity*, ACM Transactions on Computational Logic 10(1),
  2009, doi:10.1145/1459010.1459012 (p. 2:11;
  [preprint](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~ynm/papers/ac.pdf)). There g(a, b) is the least
  number of parallel stages, starting from {0, 1, a, b} and applying +, −, integer
  quotient and remainder to all available pairs, after which gcd(a, b) is available. They ask whether
  g(αₙ, βₙ) > C log αₙ along some sequence with αₙ → ∞. Since rem(β, α) appears at the
  first stage, g(α, β) ≤ 1 + G(α, rem(β, α)) = O(log α / log log α) uniformly in β, so
  no such sequence exists. This short consequence is proved in the paper, not formalised.

### What remains open

**The Main Conjecture for algorithms.** The result shows that a proof of it must use
properties beyond uniformity. It stays open because the construction gives no
remainder-only algorithm, deterministic or nondeterministic, with this cost: choosing
which two stored residues to combine is not charged, and a program would still need to
implement reusable access to the growing stored prefix within the claimed cost.

Recursive (finite nondeterministic first-order programs and the book's recursive

- **The finite recurrence.** The core is `sizeCost_step` (in FiniteBound). Its
  hypotheses concern natural-number size parameters, not an assumed complexity bound.
- **The bound.** Arithmetic discharges those hypotheses, and UpperBound proves the
  resulting bound by strong induction.
- **The logarithms.** Asymptotic compares integer logarithms with real logarithms.
- **The leading coefficient.** SharpBound reuses `sizeCost_step` with tunable prefix
  and recursive-size parameters. The original Big-O theorem supplies boundedness;
  an upper-limit argument gives 6, and rounding limits preserve it for general inputs.
- **The conjectures.** Conjecture turns the bounds into the refutations;
  SharpCertificates combines the refined bound with the certificate reductions.
- **Non-vacuity.** LowerBounds shows that the refuted complexities are not small for
  trivial reasons: certificates exist, so none is the empty infimum 0; they are at least
  G; and the coprimeness complexity is Ω(log log N) on almost all coprime pairs.

  about intrinsic complexities defined directly from certificates, represented by their
  facts; the paper's fact-representation lemma relates this to the book's finite partial

## Program family

For each p ≥ 1, a nondeterministic register program over (ℕ, rem, eq0) with 4ᵖ + 5
work registers and two read-only input registers (4ᵖ + 7 in all), and no recursion
stack, has worst-case remainder cost at most (3/p + o(1)) log₂ a, uniformly in b ≤ a.
Cost is the minimum number of remainder calls over convergent computations; every
convergent computation returns the gcd, and every input has one. Thus, for every
ε > 0, there is a fixed program with cost at most ε log₂ a + Bε on all inputs
a ≥ b ≥ 1. The paper proves this in the section "Fixed programs with arbitrarily small
coefficients". **This does not give one sublogarithmic program**: the program depends
on ε, and the Main Conjecture allows its lower-bound coefficient to depend on the
program.

The construction keeps a fixed-length Euclidean prefix and the contractions. Once the
modulus m is small, it computes a mod m and b mod m and finishes with two Euclidean
loops in place. The size thresholds appear only in the proof; the program does not
compute them.

Lean checks the result in two of the book's models of computation.

- **Register programs** (§2F, with the nondeterminism of §2E): `TailSource.Step` has a
  finite control line and the work registers; the two input registers are never
  written, and there is no stack. Every command acts on single registers: an assignment
  u_d := rem(u_i, u_j), a branch on eq0(u_i) choosing a candidate, the branches
  eq0(rem(a, u_i)) and eq0(rem(b, u_i)), and halting. The generator used in the cost
  proofs (`TailProgram.Run`) can also relabel all registers at once for free;
  `TailSource.run_trace_relabel` removes this, replaying a relabelled run on the
  physical registers with the same remainder calls and no extra registers.
- **Recursive programs** (§§2A, 2E): `TailRecursive` writes the same program one
  function per line, as in the book's reduction of iteration to tail recursion (§2C).
  The function run takes the inputs and the work registers; its bodies are
  run(u⃗[d := rem(u_i, u_j)]) for each choice of i, j, d, and accept(a, b, u_i) for each
  i. `TailRecursive.program_tailRecursive` proves that the head and every body
  have only tail-position calls: call arguments, remainder operands and conditional
  guards contain no internal calls. Operationally, `TailRecursive.stacks_bounded`
  proves that on the book's machine the control and value stacks together never hold
  more than max(w + 6, 17) entries (instructions and values), for any input and however
  long the computation runs; closure environments have at most w + 2 entries each.
  `TailRecursive.executes_iff_eval`
  matches its evaluations exactly, in value and remainder cost, with executions of the
  register program. For every program in the language of `Recursive` (terms with
  variables, remainder, a zero-test conditional and calls of internal symbols, each
  with finitely many bodies), `Recursive.Machine.eval_iff_steps` proves exact-cost
  equivalence with a specialisation of the book's two-stack machine (Table 1, p. 75):
  arguments are evaluated right to left, zero tests and branches are separate
  transitions, and only remainder transitions are charged.

The result concerns the minimum successful cost of nondeterministic programs. It does
not settle the book's Problem x2G.6, which asks whether every recursive function in
the pointed Euclidean structure is tail recursive.

<details>
<summary>Model conventions</summary>

Remainder evaluation requires a positive divisor, so Lean's total `Nat.mod` at zero is
never used as a successful primitive call. Incorrect candidates loop; divergent or stuck
computations do not count as successful. The result requires one successful computation
on every input and correctness of every terminating one, without fairness or
termination of all branches. Cost counts only remainder calls, including the two final
verification calls, not machine steps or the work of finding successful choices.
Positive scaling preserves successful runs and their costs (`TailProgram.Run.scale`).
Machine closures implement substitution; programs cannot inspect them as data.

The related-work discussion in the paper compares Pratt's program as presented in
Theorem 2E.2 and Corollary 6C.7 of the book. We did not obtain Pratt's unpublished
manuscript, and make no claim of exhaustive coverage or priority.

</details>
