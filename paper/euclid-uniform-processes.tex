\begin{abstract}
Van den Dries and Moschovakis conjectured that the Euclidean algorithm is
optimal, up to a constant factor, among the algorithms that compute the
greatest common divisor from the remainder function. Their homomorphism method
gives lower bounds for all uniform processes, but for this problem it has given only
$\Omega(\log\log a)$. We show that every pair $a>b\geq1$ has a remainder-only
derivation of $\gcd(a,b)$ with $O(\log a/\log\log a)$ remainder calls and
leading coefficient $6+o(1)$: one stored Euclidean prefix lets three-call
contractions shrink every modulus. Hence the intrinsic remainder-call
complexity of the gcd and of coprimeness is $O(\log a/\log\log a)$, and the
homomorphism method cannot reach $\Omega(\log a)$. This refutes Conjecture~3.4
of Moschovakis's 2012 notes and the version (4F-5) of the Main Conjecture in
his book. In the other direction, $\Omega(\log\log a)$ holds for almost all coprime pairs.
For each $\varepsilon>0$ we also give a fixed finite nondeterministic program
with remainder cost at most $\varepsilon\log a+B_\varepsilon$ on all inputs
$a\geq b\geq1$; the program depends on $\varepsilon$. The Main Conjecture for
algorithms remains open. The results are formalized in Lean~4; the cited
general theory of uniform processes is not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Van den Dries and Moschovakis asked whether the Euclidean algorithm is optimal
among its peers, the algorithms that compute the gcd from the remainder
function \cite{vddm2004}. Moschovakis's Main Conjecture asserts that for every
algorithm $\alpha$ from $\rem,\eq_0$ computing $\gcd(x,y)$ there is an $r>0$
such that $\alpha$ makes more than $r\log x$ remainder calls on infinitely many
pairs $x>y\geq1$ \cite[p.~2]{moschovakis2019}; here $\eq_0$ is the zero test.
Throughout, ``the book'' means Moschovakis's monograph
\cite{moschovakis2019}, and logarithms are to base $2$.

\paragraph{Derivations.}
A \emph{derivation} from $a,b$ is a finite list that starts with $a,b$ and in
which each further entry is $\rem(x,y)$ for earlier entries $x,y$ with $y>0$.
Each further entry is one \emph{instruction}, that is, one remainder call.
Entries already in the list are \emph{available} and may be reused; no
constants and no other operations are supplied. Let $G(a,b)$ be the least number of instructions in a
derivation from $a,b$ that contains $\gcd(a,b)$, and let $D(a,b)=G(a,b)$ for
coprime inputs. Thus $G(a,0)=0$ and $D(a,1)=0$. Arithmetic used in the proofs,
such as subtraction, sorting or comparison, only selects instructions; it is
never itself an instruction.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Uniformly for integers $a>b\geq1$,
\[
G(a,b)=O\left(\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}\right).
\]
In particular this bound holds for $D(a,b)$ when $\gcd(a,b)=1$.
\end{theorem}
As usual, the asymptotic assertion concerns sufficiently large $a$; a bound
valid at every positive input is $O(1+\log(2+a)/\log\log(4+a))$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:certificates}, the intrinsic remainder-call
complexity of coprimeness equals $D$ on positive coprime pairs, and that of the
gcd lies between $G$ and $G+2$. Hence Theorem~\ref{thm:main} refutes
Conjecture~3.4 of \cite{moschovakis2012} and version (4F-5) of the Main
Conjecture (Corollary~\ref{cor:refute}).

\paragraph{Structures and intrinsic complexity.}
Write $\mathrm{tt}$ and $\mathrm{ff}$ for the truth values, and
$\eq_c(x)=\mathrm{tt}$ if $x=c$ and $\eq_c(x)=\mathrm{ff}$ otherwise. We use
the book's partial remainder operation: its second argument must be nonzero.
Write
$\mathcal N_0=(\mathbb N,\rem,\eq_0)$ and
$\mathcal N_\varepsilon=(\mathbb N,\rem,\eq_0,\eq_1)$; compare
\cite[(1C-10), pp.~24, 142--143]{moschovakis2019}. The structure
$\mathcal N_0$ is not the book's $\mathbf N_0$, whose expansion
$\mathbf N_0[\div]$ appears below. Coprimeness is the relation
$x\coprime y\iff x,y\geq1\wedge\gcd(x,y)=1$
\cite[\S1A]{moschovakis2019}, \cite{moschovakis2012}. Informally, a
\emph{certificate} for $f$ at $\vec x$ in a structure $\mathcal A$ is a finite
partial substructure of $\mathcal A$ generated by $\vec x$ on which every
homomorphism into $\mathcal A$ respects $f$ at $\vec x$; the definitions of
certificates and intrinsic complexities are in
\cite[\S\S1D, 4C--4E]{moschovakis2019} and are used in
Section~\ref{sec:certificates}. Write $\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal A,f,\vec x)$
for the least number of remainder facts in a certificate for $f$ at $\vec x$
in $\mathcal A$. Uniform processes are the book's abstract model of
algorithms from given primitives; every deterministic or nondeterministic
recursive program induces one \cite{moschovakis2019}.

\paragraph{Background.}
The best known lower bound, $\Omega(\log\log a)$, is a logarithm below the
conjectured $\Omega(\log a)$. For a recursive program deciding
coprimeness from $=$, $<$, $+$, truncated subtraction, $\mathrm{iq}$ and $\rem$,
the depth of nested calls to these primitives exceeds $\tfrac1{10}\log\log a$ on
infinitely many coprime pairs \cite[Theorem~A]{vddm2004}; see also
\cite{vddm2009}. The book proves this as an intrinsic bound in
$\mathbf N_0[\div]=(\mathbb N,0,1,=,<,+,\dot{-},\mathrm{iq},\rem)$:
$\mathrm{depth}_{\coprime}(\mathbf N_0[\div],a,b)>\tfrac1{10}\log\log a$
for every ``difficult pair'', a class that contains every solution of Pell's
equation \cite[Theorem~6C.5, p.~187]{moschovakis2019}. Being intrinsic, the
bound holds for all uniform processes, nondeterministic ones included.

A certificate in $\mathcal N_\varepsilon$ becomes one in $\mathbf N_0[\div]$
when each $\eq_0$ or $\eq_1$ fact is replaced by an equality with the constant
$0$ or $1$, and its depth then exceeds its number of remainder facts by at most
a constant. Hence
$\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\coprime,a,b)=\Omega(\log\log a)$ on
all difficult pairs. Pratt's nondeterministic algorithm
\cite[Theorem~2E.2 and Problems x2E.17--x2E.19]{moschovakis2019} matches this
$\Omega(\log\log a)$ bound up to a constant factor on successive Fibonacci numbers
\cite[Corollary~6C.7]{moschovakis2019}. By contrast, for the binary gcd
algorithm the same method gives a matching logarithmic lower bound: a recursive
program deciding coprimeness from $=$, $<$, $+$, truncated subtraction, $2x$,
$\mathrm{iq}(x,2)$ and $\mathrm{rem}(x,2)$ needs more than
$\tfrac1{10}\log(a^2-1)$ calls on the pair $(a,a^2-1)$, for every $a>2$
\cite[Theorem~B]{vddm2004}. Whether a logarithmic bound holds for
all uniform processes was left open \cite[p.~188]{moschovakis2019},
\cite[Conjecture~3.4]{moschovakis2012}.

In a different model, Bshouty showed that a random access machine with linear
operations, shifts and indirect addressing computes the gcd of two integers in
$[0,N]$ in $O(\log N/\log\log N)$ operations, by reading precomputed blocks of the binary
gcd algorithm from a table. He also proved that this is optimal: even with
rational constants, multiplication and division by constants, and the floor
function added, $\Omega(\log N/\log\log N)$ operations are necessary
\cite[Theorems~i and~ii]{bshouty1989}. The construction here uses no
operation other than remainder and no table. Its saving of a factor
$\log\log a$ comes instead from reusing one stored Euclidean prefix for every
modulus.

\paragraph{The conjectures refuted.}
Moschovakis's 2012 notes state:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Conjecture 3.4} \cite{moschovakis2012}. There is a rational number
$r>0$ such that for infinitely many $a\geq b\geq1$,
$\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\coprime,a,b)\geq r\log a$.
\end{quote}
The book formulates one precise version of the Main Conjecture
\cite[p.~151]{moschovakis2019}: there are a set $\mathcal D\subseteq\mathbb N^2$
and a $K>0$ such that
\[
(x,y)\in\mathcal D\ \Longrightarrow\
\calls_{\rem}(\varepsilon,x,y)\leq L(x,y)\leq K\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal A,\gcd,x,y),
\tag{4F-5}
\]
where $L(x,y)=2\log\max(x,y)$, $\mathcal A$ is the structure ($\mathcal N_0$
or $\mathcal N_\varepsilon$), and $\varepsilon$ denotes, as in the book, the
Euclidean algorithm, and $\calls_{\rem}(\varepsilon,x,y)$ is the number of
remainder calls it makes on $(x,y)$. Elsewhere, apart from the subscript of
$\mathcal N_\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon$ denotes a small positive real.
The book adds that $\mathcal D$ then satisfies its conditions (4F-3) and
(4F-4). Condition (4F-4) makes the intrinsic complexity unbounded on
$\mathcal D$, so the intended $\mathcal D$ is infinite; for finite $\mathcal D$
the statement would be trivial. Dropping the first inequality and asking only
for an infinite $\mathcal D$ gives a weaker statement, and we refute that
weaker statement.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:refute}
The intrinsic complexities
\[
\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\coprime,a,b),\qquad
\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_0,\gcd,a,b),\qquad
\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\gcd,a,b)
\]
are $O(\log a/\log\log a)$, uniformly for $a\geq b\geq1$. Consequently Conjecture~3.4 of
\cite{moschovakis2012} is false, and so is the version (4F-5) of the Main
Conjecture with $\mathcal A=\mathcal N_0$ or $\mathcal A=\mathcal N_\varepsilon$:
for every $r>0$ only finitely many pairs $a\geq b\geq1$ satisfy
$\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\coprime,a,b)\geq r\log a$, and for every
$K>0$ only finitely many pairs $(x,y)\in\mathbb N^2$ satisfy
$L(x,y)\leq K\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal A,\gcd,x,y)$.
\end{corollary}
The corollary follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:main} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:certificates}; for noncoprime pairs, the bound on the
coprimeness complexity comes from Remark~\ref{rem:noncoprime}. For (4F-5) it covers all pairs in
$\mathbb N^2$: the gcd is symmetric, and a pair with a zero entry is certified by
the single fact $\eq_0(0)=\mathrm{tt}$, with no remainder facts. The
corollary is formalized, and so are refutations of the literal statements:
Conjecture~3.4 with $r$ rational and with infinitely many values of $a$ (not
merely of pairs), and (4F-5) for $\mathcal N_\varepsilon$ with a set
$\mathcal D$ on which the intrinsic complexity is unbounded.

Theorem~4F.1 \cite[pp.~149--150]{moschovakis2019} supplies a single uniform
process that attains these pointwise intrinsic bounds. Hence some uniform
processes of $\mathcal N_\varepsilon$ decide coprimeness and compute the gcd
with $O(\log a/\log\log a)$ remainder calls on all pairs $a\geq b\geq1$. This settles
the possibility
raised after Corollary~6C.7 \cite[p.~188]{moschovakis2019}: the Main Conjecture
does not hold for all uniform processes of $\mathcal N_\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{The limit of the homomorphism method.}
The homomorphism method proves lower bounds on intrinsic complexities. These
bound every uniform process that computes the function: for any of the book's
complexity measures $\mu$, such as $\calls_{\rem}$, if $\alpha$ computes
$f$ in $\mathcal A$ with cost $\mu(\alpha,\vec x)$ on $\vec x$, then
$\mu(\mathcal A,f,\vec x)\leq\mu(\alpha,\vec x)$, in particular for every deterministic or nondeterministic recursive program
\cite[Proposition~4E.1, Corollary~4E.2]{moschovakis2019}. The Homomorphism
Test \cite[Lemma~4E.3]{moschovakis2019} proves a lower bound on
$\mu(\mathcal A,f,\vec x)$ itself, by showing that every generated diagram
below the proposed cost admits a homomorphism that fails to preserve the
answer. Corollary~\ref{cor:refute} caps every such argument.

\begin{corollary}[Limit of the homomorphism method]\label{cor:method}
Let $\mathcal A=\mathcal N_0$ or $\mathcal A=\mathcal N_\varepsilon$, and
let $g(a,b)$ be any lower bound for
$\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\coprime,a,b)$ or for
$\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal A,\gcd,a,b)$. Then $g(a,b)=O(\log a/\log\log a)$, uniformly for
$a\geq b\geq1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Immediate from Corollary~\ref{cor:refute}.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:method}, no lower bound on intrinsic complexity shows that every algorithm from $\rem,\eq_0$ computing the gcd
makes at least $r\log a$ remainder calls on infinitely many pairs. A proof of
the Main Conjecture must use properties of algorithms that uniform processes
need not have, such as being induced by a finite program, or determinism.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Fixed programs.}
There is also an algorithmic consequence with a different quantifier order.
Section~\ref{sec:programs} constructs fixed finite nondeterministic recursive
programs with arbitrarily small logarithmic remainder-call coefficients,
uniformly over all inputs. The programs use a fixed-length prefix and ordinary
recursive calls. Pratt's algorithm is known to attain $O(\log\log a)$ on
successive Fibonacci numbers; here the logarithmic coefficient holds on all
inputs and is made small by enlarging the program.

\par\paragraph{Idea of the proof.}
Let $a>b$ be coprime with $a\leq N=2^n$, and store the Euclidean remainders
$r_0=a,r_1=b,\ldots,r_h$, where $h=\lceil\sqrt n\rceil$. If one of them is
at most the early threshold $N^{3/4}$, recurse on that consecutive pair.
Otherwise the consecutive pairs of the prefix stay distinct modulo every
$m>N^{1/3}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:prefix}), so the residues $r_i\bmod m$ take at
least $\sqrt h$ values, and three remainder calls produce a new positive
modulus below $m/(\sqrt h-1)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:gap}). Repeating this from
$m=r_h$ reaches the target modulus $N^{1/3}$ within $O(n/\log n)$ calls, and
two recursive gcd computations on inputs at most $N^{1/3}$ finish. The
resulting recurrence \eqref{eq:recurrence} has solution
$O(n/\log n)=O(\log a/\log\log a)$.

\section{Normalization}\label{sec:normalization}
Write $g=\gcd(a,b)>0$. For positive $y$,
\[
\rem(gx,gy)=g\rem(x,y).
\]
Every generated value is divisible by $g$. Consequently scaling a derivation,
or dividing all its values by $g$, preserves its length and the validity of
every instruction. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalize}
G(a,b)=D(a/g,b/g).
\end{equation}
Define
\[
T(n)=\max_{0\leq b\leq a\leq 2^n,\ a>0}G(a,b).
\]
Each $G(a,b)$ is defined, since the Euclidean remainders form a derivation
that contains $\gcd(a,b)$, so $T(n)$ is a finite maximum. By \eqref{eq:normalize} it
suffices to bound $D$; the cases $a=b$, $b=0$ and $b=1$ need no instructions.

\section{Three-call contractions}\label{sec:contractions}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gap}
If $m>0$ is available and a finite collection $S$ of available values occupies
$t\geq2$ residue classes modulo $m$, at most three instructions generate $w$
with
\[
0<w<\frac{m}{t-1}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Sort the distinct residues. Some adjacent pair $u<v$ has gap
$\delta=v-u<m/(t-1)$, because the sum of the $t-1$ adjacent gaps is less than
$m$. Each selected residue is $\rem(x,m)$ for some $x\in S$, so it costs one
instruction. If $u=0$, use $v$. If $0<u\leq\delta$, use $u$. Otherwise
$0<\delta<u$ and $v=u+\delta$, so $\rem(v,u)=\delta$. This uses at most three
instructions.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Euclidean-prefix lemma]\label{lem:prefix}
Let $r_0=a>r_1=b>\cdots>r_h>0$ be a Euclidean prefix of a coprime pair, with
$r_{i-1}=q_i r_i+r_{i+1}$ and $q_i\geq1$ for $1\leq i<h$. For every integer
$m>2a/r_h$, the
$h$ pairs
\[
(r_i\bmod m,r_{i+1}\bmod m),\qquad 0\leq i<h,
\]
are distinct. The prefix therefore occupies $t$ residue classes with $t^2\geq h$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose the pairs at $i<j$ coincide. Set
\[
M=\prod_{\ell=i+1}^{j}\begin{pmatrix}q_\ell&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&E\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\binom{r_i}{r_{i+1}}=M\binom{r_j}{r_{j+1}}.
\]
Right multiplication by
$\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}q&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ sends
$(A,B,C,E)$ to $(qA+B,\,A,\,qC+E,\,C)$, starting from $(q,1,1,0)$ for a single
factor. By induction, all entries are nonnegative, $A\geq B\geq E$,
$A\geq C\geq1$, $B\geq1$, and $\det M=(-1)^{j-i}$.
Also $Ar_j\leq r_i\leq a$ and $r_j>r_h$ (as $j<h$), whence $A<a/r_h$.
Write
\[
\Delta=\det(M-I)=1+\det M-A-E.
\]
For an odd number of factors, $\Delta=-A-E<0$. By the same recursion, a
product of at least two factors has $A\geq2$ and $E\geq1$; thus for an even
positive number of factors,
$\Delta=2-A-E<0$. In either case,
\[
0<|\Delta|\leq A+E\leq2A\leq2a/r_h<m.
\]
The presumed congruence gives $(M-I)(r_j,r_{j+1})^{\mathsf T}\equiv0\pmod m$.
Multiplication by the adjugate shows that $m$ divides both $\Delta r_j$ and
$\Delta r_{j+1}$. Consecutive remainders are coprime, so B\'ezout's identity
implies $m\mid\Delta$, a contradiction. Finally, $t$ individual residue classes
give at most $t^2$ ordered pairs.
\end{proof}
Thus a stored prefix can be reused for every available modulus $m>2a/r_h$.
When $\sqrt h>1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:gap} applied to the prefix gives three
instructions that generate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contract}
0<w<\frac{m}{\sqrt h-1}.
\end{equation}
We call these three instructions a \emph{contraction} of $m$, and $w$ the new
modulus.
The universal quantifier over $m$ is essential: the modulus generated by a
contraction is not predetermined. In Lemma~\ref{lem:prefix} the prefix length
$h$ is generic; Section~\ref{sec:construction} chooses
$h=\lceil\sqrt n\rceil$, while Corollary~\ref{cor:six} and
Section~\ref{sec:programs} choose $h=k^2+1$.

\section{Construction and recurrence}\label{sec:construction}
Fix an integer $n>16$ and put $N=2^n$ and $h=\lceil\sqrt n\rceil$, so that
$h>4$; this $h$ is the prefix length to which Lemma~\ref{lem:prefix} will be
applied. After normalization we may assume that $a>b>1$ are coprime and
$a\leq N$. The derivation has three phases.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] Store a Euclidean prefix of length at most $h$. If it reaches a
positive remainder at most the \emph{early threshold} $N^{3/4}$, recurse on
that smaller consecutive pair; this gives \eqref{eq:early}.
\item[(ii)] Otherwise contract the modulus with \eqref{eq:contract}, starting
at $m=r_h$, until $0<m\leq N^{1/3}$, the \emph{target modulus}.
\item[(iii)] Finish with two recursive gcd derivations on inputs at most $m$;
phases (ii) and (iii) together give \eqref{eq:late}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Phase (i): the prefix.}
Generate the Euclidean remainders $r_2,r_3,\ldots$, one instruction each, and stop as
soon as the most recent value is positive and at most $N^{3/4}$, or once $r_h$
is available. The test is also applied to the input $r_1=b$, so if
$b\leq N^{3/4}$ no instruction is used. This preliminary work costs at most
$h$ instructions. Since the inputs are coprime, Euclid's algorithm reaches the positive
remainder $1$ before it can reach zero, so the rule stops before a zero
remainder appears.

If a positive remainder $c\leq N^{3/4}$ is encountered, generate the next
remainder $s<c$ with one more instruction. The pair $(c,s)$ is coprime. If $s=0$, then
$c=1$ is already available; otherwise both inputs to the remaining derivation
are at most $2^{\lceil3n/4\rceil}$. This gives the bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:early}
h+1+T(\lceil3n/4\rceil).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Phase (ii): contractions.}
Otherwise $r_h>N^{3/4}$, so $2a/r_h<2N^{1/4}$. For $n>12$,
$N^{1/3}>2N^{1/4}$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:prefix} applies to every available
$m>N^{1/3}$. Starting at $m=r_h$, repeatedly replace $m$ by the value $w$ of
\eqref{eq:contract} until $0<m\leq N^{1/3}$. Set
\[
\kappa_n=\sqrt h-1>1,\qquad
J_n=\left\lceil\frac{2n/3}{\log\kappa_n}\right\rceil.
\]
There are at most $J_n$ contractions: if $J_n$ have occurred then
$m<N/\kappa_n^{J_n}\leq N^{1/3}$. Since
$\log\kappa_n=\tfrac14\log n+O(1)$, their cost is $3J_n=O(n/\log n)$.

\paragraph{Phase (iii): two recursive derivations.}
The small $m$ need not be coprime to either input. Generate
$u=\rem(a,m)$ and $v=\rem(b,m)$, then concatenate derivations of
$d=\gcd(m,u)$ and $e=\gcd(d,v)$; no gcd-oracle instruction is used. Since
$m\leq2^{\lceil n/3\rceil}$ and $G$ is symmetric, each has a remainder-only
derivation of length at most $T(\lceil n/3\rceil)$. Each sub-derivation
starts from values already in the list ($m,u$, and then $d,v$), so it can be
appended unchanged. The gcd identities give
\[
e=\gcd(\gcd(m,a\bmod m),b\bmod m)
 =\gcd(m,\gcd(a,b))=1.
\]
The cost in this case is at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:late}
h+3J_n+2+2T(\lceil n/3\rceil).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The recurrence.}
The two case estimates \eqref{eq:early} and \eqref{eq:late} and normalization
imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recurrence}
T(n)\leq\max\{T(\lceil3n/4\rceil)+O(\sqrt n),\;
2T(\lceil n/3\rceil)+O(n/\log n)\}.
\end{equation}
All implied constants are independent of the inputs.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
For $n\geq2$ let $f(n)=n/\log n$. The ratios
\[
\frac{f(\lceil3n/4\rceil)}{f(n)}\longrightarrow\frac34,
\qquad
\frac{2f(\lceil n/3\rceil)}{f(n)}\longrightarrow\frac23
\]
show that, beyond a fixed threshold, $f(\lceil3n/4\rceil)\leq\tfrac78f(n)$ and
$2f(\lceil n/3\rceil)\leq\tfrac56f(n)$. Both overheads in
\eqref{eq:recurrence} are at most $c_0f(n)$ for a fixed $c_0$. Choose the
induction threshold above $16$, where the recurrence holds, and large enough
that both recursive indices are at least $2$ and strictly less than $n$. Choose
$C\geq8c_0$, increased to cover the finitely many values below the threshold.
Strong induction then gives $T(n)\leq Cf(n)$: the two bounds are
$(7C/8+c_0)f(n)$ and $(5C/6+c_0)f(n)$, both at most $Cf(n)$.
Finally take $n=\lceil\log a\rceil$. This also gives $G(a,b)=o(\log a)$
uniformly as $a\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The leading coefficient}
The same construction allows a more precise bound without changing either
arithmetic lemma.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:six}
For every $\varepsilon>0$, there is an $a_0$ such that for every
$a\geq a_0$ and $0\leq b\leq a$,
\[
G(a,b)\leq(6+\varepsilon)\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}.
\]
Equivalently, the uniform upper bound has coefficient $6+o(1)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Fix $0<c<1/2$ and $0<\beta<1/2$, and put $\alpha=1-\beta/2$.
For sufficiently large $n$, choose the prefix length $h$ afresh, together
with auxiliary parameters, as
\[
p=\lfloor c\log n\rfloor,\qquad k=2^p,\qquad h=k^2+1,
\qquad J=\lceil n/p\rceil.
\]
Use $N^\alpha$ as the early threshold and $N^\beta$ as the target modulus,
where $N=2^n$. In the late case $r_h>N^\alpha$, so
$2a/r_h<2N^{1-\alpha}=2N^{\beta/2}<N^\beta$ for sufficiently large $n$, and
the prefix lemma applies to every modulus above $N^\beta$. Since $k^2<h$, the prefix occupies at least $k+1$ residue classes,
so each contraction reduces the modulus by a factor greater than $k$.
Moreover $k^J\geq N$, so $J$ contractions suffice.
Writing $f(n)=n/\log n$, we have
\[
h=O(n^{2c})=o(f(n)),\qquad
3J=\left(\frac3c+o(1)\right)f(n).
\]
The two branches therefore give
\[
T(n)\leq\max\left\{
 T(\lceil\alpha n\rceil)+o(f(n)),\quad
 2T(\lceil\beta n\rceil)+\left(\frac3c+o(1)\right)f(n)
\right\}.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} implies that
$L=\limsup_{n\to\infty}T(n)/f(n)$ is finite and nonnegative.
For each fixed $q>0$, the ratio $f(\lceil qn\rceil)/f(n)$ tends to $q$.
Taking upper limits in the recurrence yields
\[
L\leq\max\{\alpha L,\ 2\beta L+3/c\}.
\]
If the first term is the larger, $L\leq\alpha L$ forces $L=0$, as
$\alpha<1$; otherwise $L(1-2\beta)\leq3/c$. In either case
$L(1-2\beta)\leq3/c$. First let $\beta\downarrow0$ and then $c\uparrow1/2$ to obtain
$L\leq6$. Finally, $n=\lceil\log a\rceil$ satisfies
$f(n)\sim\log a/\log\log a$, proving the assertion. All upper limits concern
the worst-case function $T$, so the resulting threshold is independent of $b$.
\end{proof}
No optimality of the coefficient $6$ is asserted. The parameters of
Section~\ref{sec:construction} suffice for the simpler Big-O proof above.

\section{Certificates and uniform processes}\label{sec:certificates}
This section shows that certificates for coprimeness correspond to derivations
of $1$, and certificates for the gcd to derivations of the gcd with two extra
remainder facts. A derivation determines a finite generated partial
substructure by recording its remainder equations. Repeated equations can be
discarded.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:certificates}
For coprime $a,b\geq1$,
$\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\coprime,a,b)=D(a,b)$. For
$(a,b)\neq(0,0)$,
\[
G(a,b)\leq\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_0,\gcd,a,b)\leq G(a,b)+2,
\]
and the same bounds hold for $\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\gcd,a,b)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $\gcd{=}1$ for the relation $\gcd(x,y)=1$ on all of $\mathbb N^2$ (unlike
$\coprime$, it holds at $(1,0)$). A derivation of $1$
together with $\eq_1(1)=\mathrm{tt}$ certifies the relation: every common
divisor of the images of the inputs divides every transported generated
value, hence divides $1$. Conversely, without a positive $\eq_1$ fact the
map $x\mapsto2x$ preserves every recorded remainder and zero-test fact, as
well as every negative one-test fact. It cannot preserve coprimeness. A
certificate therefore contains a positive $\eq_1$ fact, hence the value $1$. Enumerating a finite generated diagram
in generation order gives a derivation using no more calls than the number
of remainder facts. Consequently, on coprime inputs,
writing $\calls(\cdot)$ for the number of all primitive facts of a
certificate, not only remainder facts,
\[
\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\gcd{=}1,a,b)=D(a,b),\qquad
\calls(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\gcd{=}1,a,b)=D(a,b)+1.
\]
For the book's relation $\coprime$, which also requires $a,b\geq1$, it
suffices to add the facts $\eq_0(a)=\mathrm{ff}$ and $\eq_0(b)=\mathrm{ff}$.
Conversely, when $a\coprime b$, every certificate for
$\coprime$ also certifies $\gcd(a,b)=1$. So for coprime $a,b\geq1$ the
remainder count is unchanged,
$\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\coprime,a,b)=D(a,b)$, and the
total count is at most $D(a,b)+3$.

For gcd as a function, the certificate must reach its output $g$, giving a
lower bound of $G(a,b)$. For an upper bound, append
\[
\rem(a,g)=z,\qquad \rem(b,g)=z,\qquad \eq_0(z)=\mathrm{tt},
\]
where the generated $z$ is zero. Every homomorphism $\pi$ has $\pi(z)=0$ and
$\pi(g)>0$ (by definedness of either appended remainder). Thus $\pi(g)$
divides both input images. Conversely every common divisor of those images
divides $\pi(g)$ by induction over the derivation. Therefore, for
$(a,b)\neq(0,0)$,
\[
G(a,b)\leq\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_0,\gcd,a,b)\leq G(a,b)+2.
\]
The lower-bound argument applies to every gcd certificate, $\eq_1$ facts
included, and allowing $\eq_1$ facts can only lower the count, so the upper
bound transfers to $\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\gcd,a,b)$.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}\label{rem:noncoprime}
At $(0,0)$ the output is an input and the empty certificate suffices.
If the gcd is taken as a function on positive inputs only, the facts
$\eq_0(a)=\mathrm{ff}$ and $\eq_0(b)=\mathrm{ff}$ may be appended. For a noncoprime input, adding $\eq_1(g)=\mathrm{ff}$ to the gcd
certificate certifies noncoprimeness, so the sublogarithmic remainder-call
bound holds for both truth values of coprimeness as well as for the gcd. The
overhead is a fixed additive constant and does not change the leading
coefficient, so Corollary~\ref{cor:six} holds for all three complexities of
Corollary~\ref{cor:refute} on positive inputs.
\end{remark}

\section{An almost-all lower bound}\label{sec:loglog}
The difficult pairs of \cite[Theorem~6C.5]{moschovakis2019} give
$\Omega(\log\log a)$ on a sparse set. An elementary Diophantine argument gives
the same order for almost all coprime pairs, simultaneously for every
derivation.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:loglog}
For every $d\geq1$ and $N\geq3$, at most $16(2+\ln N)N^{2-2^{-d}}$ coprime
pairs $2\leq b<a\leq N$ satisfy $D(a,b)\leq d$. Hence
$D(a,b)\geq(1-o(1))\log\log N$ for all but $o(N^2)$ coprime pairs below $N$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $B\geq1$ call $(a,b)$ \emph{$B$-regular} if $|qb-pa|\geq a/(Bq)$ whenever
$q\geq1$, $p\in\mathbb Z$ and $qb\neq pa$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Small values have large coefficients.} Every generated value is
an integer form $v=ua+wb$. If $(a,b)$ is $B$-regular and $v>0$, then
$v\geq a/(B(|u|+|w|))$: for $w\neq0$ apply regularity with $q=|w|$, and for
$w=0$ note $v\geq a$.
\item \textbf{Coefficients grow at most doubly exponentially.} Let $H_t$ bound
$|u|+|w|$ over the values available after $t$ instructions, so $H_0=1$. An
instruction $r=\rem(x,y)$, with $x=sy+r$, has quotient $s\leq a/y\leq BH_t$,
hence $H_{t+1}\leq H_t+BH_t^2\leq2BH_t^2$ and $H_t\leq(2B)^{2^t-1}$.
\item \textbf{Regular pairs need long derivations.} Producing $1$ within $d$
instructions forces $1\geq a/(BH_d)$, that is $a\leq(2B)^{2^d}/2$.
\item \textbf{Few pairs are irregular.} Suppose $0<|qb-pa|<a/(Bq)$. Dividing
$p,q$ by their gcd preserves this, and it forces $q<a/B\leq N/B$ and
$0\leq p\leq q$. For each $q$ there are at most $2q$ values of $p$ and
$2N/(Bq)$ values of $r=qb-pa$. Given $p,q,r$ with $\gcd(p,q)=1$, $a$ lies in
one residue class modulo $q$, which leaves at most $N/q+1$ choices, and $b$ is
then determined. So at most $(4N^2/B)(2+\ln N)$ pairs below $N$ are irregular.
\item \textbf{Dyadic ranges.} For $X/2<a\leq X$ take $B=X^{2^{-d}}/2$. If
$B\geq1$, a regular pair with $D\leq d$ would have $a\leq X/2$, so every pair
in the range with $D\leq d$ is irregular. That gives at most
$8(2+\ln X)X^{2-2^{-d}}$ pairs, and the same bound is trivial when $B<1$.
Summing over $X=N,N/2,\ldots$ gives the first claim.
\end{itemize}
For the second claim take $d=\lfloor\log_2\ln N-2\log_2\ln\ln N\rfloor$. Then
$N^{-2^{-d}}\leq\exp(-(\ln\ln N)^2)$, so the bound is $o(N^2)$. \qedhere
\end{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:loglog} is formalized, with a slightly larger constant;
see Appendix~\ref{app:lean}.
The saving $2^{-d}$ in the exponent of $N^{2-2^{-d}}$ shrinks as $d$ grows, so this argument cannot reach the
scale $\log N/\log\log N$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, let alone $\log N$.

\section{Fixed programs with arbitrarily small coefficients}\label{sec:programs}

For a nondeterministic program $P$, let $c_P(a,b)$ be the minimum number of
remainder calls among its convergent computations. We require at least one
such computation on each input and require every convergent computation to
return the gcd. These are the conventions of
\cite[(2E-2), (2E-5)]{moschovakis2019}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:programs}
For each integer $p\geq1$, there is a finite nondeterministic recursive program
$P_p$ over $(\mathbb N,\rem,\eq_0)$ computing the gcd such that
\[
\limsup_{a\to\infty}\ \max_{1\leq b\leq a}
\frac{c_{P_p}(a,b)}{\log a}\leq\frac3p.
\]
Its generator, defined below, has $W=4^p+5$ individual parameters, and no
internal function has larger arity. Consequently, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are a fixed
program $P$ and a constant $B\geq0$ such that
\[
\forall a\geq b\geq1,\qquad c_P(a,b)\leq\varepsilon\log a+B.
\]
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The program.}
Fix $W\geq3$ and write $\vec x=(x_0,\ldots,x_{W-1})$ and
$\vec x[i\leftarrow E]$ for the tuple with its $i$th expression replaced by
$E$. The generator is given by the finite alternatives
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{choose}(\vec x)&=x_i &&(0\leq i<W),\tag{C}\\
\operatorname{gen}(\vec x)&=\operatorname{choose}(\vec x),\tag{G1}\\
\operatorname{gen}(\vec x)&=\operatorname{gen}
  (\vec x[i\leftarrow\rem(\operatorname{choose}(\vec x),
                               \operatorname{choose}(\vec x))])
  &&(0\leq i<W),\tag{G2}\\
\operatorname{gen}(\vec x)&=\operatorname{gen}
  (\vec x[i\leftarrow\operatorname{gen}(\vec x)]) &&(0\leq i<W),\tag{G3}\\
\operatorname{gen}(\vec x)&=\operatorname{gen}
  (\operatorname{choose}(\vec x),\ldots,\operatorname{choose}(\vec x)).\tag{G4}
\end{align*}
There are $W$ independent selector calls in (G4). Tuple notation and
indices are syntax abbreviations: after fixing $W$, each line expands into
ordinary first-order terms with fixed arities. No tuple, index, numeric
constant, or update operation is supplied as runtime data or a primitive.
The nested generator in (G3) is evaluated once; its caller's
other parameters remain available when it returns. The recursion stack can
grow, so $W$ is an arity bound, not a total storage bound.

Add $\operatorname{loop}(z)=\operatorname{loop}(z)$ and define
$\operatorname{accept}(a,b,g)$ by nested zero-test conditionals: if $g=0$,
loop; otherwise test $\rem(a,g)=0$ and then $\rem(b,g)=0$, returning $g$ if
both tests succeed and looping otherwise. The head is
\[
P_W(a,b)=\text{if }\eq_0(b)\text{ then }a\text{ else }
\operatorname{accept}(a,b,\operatorname{gen}(a,b,a,\ldots,a)).
\]
The four internal symbols have arities $W,W,3,1$ (for $\operatorname{choose}$,
$\operatorname{gen}$, $\operatorname{accept}$, $\operatorname{loop}$). Every common divisor of
the generator's parameters divides its output, by induction on a finite
evaluation. Thus a candidate is divisible by $\gcd(a,b)$; successful
verification proves the reverse divisibility. Every convergent computation
therefore returns the gcd. There is always a convergent computation following
Euclid, using parameter selection and copying to retain the current pair.
For $b>0$, final verification costs exactly two remainder calls.

This syntax is a finite recursive program in the sense of
\cite[\S\S2A, 2E]{moschovakis2019}; Appendix~\ref{app:lean} records the Lean
check that its evaluation trees correspond, with equal remainder cost, to
convergent computations of a specialization of the book's recursive machine.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:programs}]
We follow the construction of Section~\ref{sec:construction}: store the
prefix, contract, then recurse twice through nested generator calls.
Take $P_p=P_W$ with $W=4^p+5$; that is, set $k=2^p$, $h=k^2+1$ (the prefix
length, as in Corollary~\ref{cor:six} but now with $p$ fixed), and $W=h+4$.
Store $r_0,\ldots,r_h$ in $h+1$
parameters, reserving three parameters for the modulus and two scratch
residues. Lemmas~\ref{lem:gap} and~\ref{lem:prefix} give a contraction by a
factor greater than $k$ in at most three remainder calls, preserving the
prefix. The program can choose precisely the operands of each contraction.

The \emph{generator cost} of $(a,b)$ is the minimum number of remainder
calls in an evaluation of $\operatorname{gen}$ that returns $\gcd(a,b)$ from
$(a,b,a,\ldots,a)$. Let $T_W(n)$ be its maximum over $a,b\leq2^n$. Euclid
gives $T_W(n)=O(n)$. To \emph{route} to $x,y$ means to apply (G4) to pass to
the tuple $(x,y,x,\ldots,x)$, at no remainder cost. Copying parameters gives
symmetry. Multiplying every value by $g=\gcd(a,b)$ turns a run on
$(a/g,b/g)$ that returns $1$ into a run on $(a,b)$ that returns $g$, with the
same remainder count. It therefore suffices to analyze coprime inputs.

Fix $0<\beta<1/2$ and put $\alpha=1-\beta/2$. Use the early threshold
$N^\alpha$ and the target modulus $N^\beta$, where $N=2^n$. If no prefix
remainder is at most $N^\alpha$, then $r_h>N^\alpha$ and
$2a/r_h<2N^{1-\alpha}<N^\beta$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
There are at least $k+1$ distinct prefix residues modulo every modulus used
before stopping, since $h>k^2$. At most $\lceil n/p\rceil$ contractions
produce a positive $m\leq N^\beta$.

In the early case, route directly to the smaller consecutive Euclidean pair.
Otherwise form $u=a\bmod m$ and $v=b\bmod m$ in the scratch positions. A nested
generator call, with its parameters routed to copies of $m,u$, returns
$d=\gcd(m,u)$ while its caller retains $v$. Replace the modulus parameter
by $d$ and route to copies of $d,v$ for the second gcd computation. The
result is $\gcd(m,a,b)=1$. All these routes and nested calls are available
in the same fixed program. Thus, eventually in $n$,
\[
T_W(n)\leq\max\left\{
 T_W(\lceil\alpha n\rceil)+h+1,\quad
 2T_W(\lceil\beta n\rceil)+h+3\lceil n/p\rceil+2
\right\}.
\]
Set $L_W=\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_W(n)/n$, finite and nonnegative by Euclid.
Since $h$ is fixed, division by $n$ and passage to upper limits gives
\[
L_W\leq\max\{\alpha L_W,\ 2\beta L_W+3/p\}.
\]
If the first term is the larger, $L_W\leq\alpha L_W$ forces $L_W=0$, as
$\alpha<1$; otherwise $(1-2\beta)L_W\leq3/p$. The program is
independent of $\beta$, so letting $\beta\downarrow0$ gives $L_W\leq3/p$.
Take $n=\lceil\log a\rceil$ and add the two verification calls. Finally,
choose $p>3/\varepsilon$ and absorb the finitely many smaller inputs in $B$.
\end{proof}

\section{Discussion and open problems}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{The gap.}
With the lower bound recalled in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, the intrinsic
complexity of coprimeness in $\mathcal N_\varepsilon$ now lies between
$\Omega(\log\log a)$, on infinitely many pairs and in fact on almost all
coprime pairs (Proposition~\ref{prop:loglog}), and $O(\log a/\log\log a)$, on
all pairs. Closing this gap is an open problem. In particular, it is not known
whether the bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is optimal for remainder-only
derivations, as the corresponding bound is in Bshouty's model.

\paragraph{Beyond the homomorphism method.}
Corollary~\ref{cor:method} does not exclude arguments that combine
homomorphisms with additional restrictions on computations.
Corollaries~\ref{cor:refute} and~\ref{cor:method} also do not settle the form
of the Main Conjecture for deterministic uniform processes
\cite[Conjecture~3.9]{moschovakis2012},
\cite[\S4H and p.~188]{moschovakis2019}.

\paragraph{Uncharged choice.}
Selecting a close pair of residues is uncharged existential choice in a
straight-line derivation. A deterministic remainder-only algorithm cannot
assume that choice is available at constant cost. Computing all prefix
residues would take about $h$ calls per contraction. The programs of
Section~\ref{sec:programs} make the selection nondeterministically, but only
with a prefix of fixed length. This leaves a further question: can one fixed
first-order recursive program store and repeatedly access a prefix whose
length grows with the input, and so attain $O(\log a/\log\log a)$ remainder
calls? Proposition~2E.1 converts each certificate into a
computation of some program; it does not supply one program for all
certificates \cite[\S2E]{moschovakis2019}. The book also distinguishes the
best uniform process from a nondeterministic program \cite[p.~150]{moschovakis2019}.
The construction therefore settles neither algorithmic version of the Main
Conjecture: the Main Conjecture for algorithms, that is, recursive programs,
deterministic or nondeterministic \cite[p.~2]{moschovakis2019}, remains open.

\paragraph{Quantifier order.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:programs} is a statement about a family of programs: the
prefix length grows when a smaller coefficient is requested, but remains fixed
as the inputs grow, and no single sublogarithmic program is obtained.
The Main Conjecture allows its lower-bound coefficient to depend on the
program, so Theorem~\ref{thm:programs} is compatible with it. Neither the
coefficient nor the arity bound is claimed optimal. Merely inlining several
remainder instructions into one term would still charge for each external
remainder call; the saving here uses the prefix lemma and recursive reduction.

\subsection{Bounds, certificates and refutations}
The theorem \lean{uniform_real_bound} states
\[
\exists C>0\ \forall a,b\in\mathbb N,\quad
4\leq a\ \wedge\ b\leq a\ \Longrightarrow\
G(a,b)\leq C\frac{\log_2 a}{\log_2(\log_2 a)}.
\]
The sharper theorem \lean{uniform_real_bound_six} states that for
every $\varepsilon>0$, eventually in $a$, the same inequality holds for all
$b\leq a$ with $C=6+\varepsilon$, and
\lean{uniform_intrinsic_bound_six} gives the corresponding statement
for the three intrinsic complexities on positive inputs.
The proof starts from a finite-store instruction semantics in which the only
instruction is remainder with a positive, available divisor. Costs are lengths
of explicit derivations, not values of an assumed recurrence. A formal conversion to
finite generated diagrams proves the gcd certificate bound with two extra
remainder facts and the bound for positive and negative coprimeness instances.
The reduction itself is also checked: on coprime inputs, the least number of
remainder facts in a certificate for $\gcd(a,b)=1$ equals $D(a,b)$, and $G$
agrees with $D$.

The Lean definitions from certificates are \lean{callsCoprimeness}
for the book's relation $\coprime$ in $\mathcal N_\varepsilon$, with positive
inputs, and \lean{callsGcd} and \lean{callsRemGcd} for the
gcd in $\mathcal N_0$ and $\mathcal N_\varepsilon$. Adding the facts
$\eq_0(a)=\mathrm{ff}$ and $\eq_0(b)=\mathrm{ff}$ is checked to turn a
certificate for $\gcd(a,b)=1$ into one for $\coprime$, so on positive coprime
pairs the complexity agrees with the reduction.

The refutation theorems state that no $r>0$ satisfies
$\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal N_\varepsilon,\coprime,a,b)\geq r\log_2 a$
for infinitely many $a\geq b\geq1$, and that no $K>0$ satisfies
$2\log_2\max(x,y)\leq K\calls_{\rem}(\mathcal A,\gcd,x,y)$ for infinitely
many pairs in $\mathbb N^2$, for either structure. The original
$O(\log a/\log\log a)$ bounds combine the certificate bounds, which bound the
intrinsic complexities by $G(a,b)+2$, with
\lean{uniform_real_bound}; \lean{eventually_calls_le} states
the resulting $o(\log)$ bound.

\subsection{The almost-all bound}
Proposition~\ref{prop:loglog} is checked in the module \lean{ShortGCD.AlmostAll},
with the constant $40(2+\ln N)$ in place of $16(2+\ln N)$: the proof takes
$B=a^{2^{-d}}/2$ separately for each pair instead of dyadic ranges.
\lean{AlmostAll.card_within_le} bounds the number of pairs
$2\leq b<a\leq N$ with a derivation of $1$ of length at most $d$ by
$40(2+\ln N)N^{2-2^{-d}}$; \lean{AlmostAll.within_iff_D_le} identifies
this with $D(a,b)\leq d$ on coprime pairs; and
\lean{AlmostAll.tendsto_card_within_loglog} proves the second claim
in the form $D(a,b)>(1-\varepsilon)\log_2\log_2N$ for all but $o(N^2)$ coprime
pairs, for every $\varepsilon\in(0,1]$.

\subsection{Fixed programs and operational correspondence}
Theorem~\ref{thm:programs} is checked in the eight modules
\lean{ShortGCD.RegisterProgram} through \lean{ShortGCD.RegisterSmallStep},
which are included in the normal build and axiom audit. The theorem
\lean{RegisterProgram.maxCost_step} proves the fixed-parameter
recurrence, and \lean{RegisterProgram.Fixed.limsup_profile_le} proves
its coefficient $3/p$. The syntax development gives finite body sets for the
four internal symbols of Section~\ref{sec:programs}. Generator runs translate
into evaluation trees with exactly the same remainder count, including final
verification. Every terminating evaluation is correct, an evaluation exists on
every input, and the quantified bound holds; for a fixed $p$, the bound
holds eventually with every coefficient greater than $3/p$.

In Lean we specialize the book's
two-stack transition table to this language and program family. Closures
represent substitution of actual values into terms; they are machine states,
not additional program data. The control stack has its top at the head, and
the value stack distinguishes individuals from booleans. Argument evaluation,
zero testing, and branching are explicit transitions. The finite evaluation
trees correspond to convergent machine computations, preserving both result
and remainder count. For the forward direction, induct on the evaluation
tree. Evaluate argument subtrees right to left, as prescribed by the machine's
composition rule, then choose the indicated body at the internal call.
Transition locality permits each subtree computation to be placed in its
calling context \cite[Table~1 and Lemma~2D.1, pp.~75--76]{moschovakis2019}.
A remainder node adds one external remainder call; a conditional evaluates
its guard, makes one uncharged zero test, and evaluates only the selected
branch. Variables and internal calls add no remainder calls. For the reverse
direction, decompose a convergent computation at the completed argument
evaluations and the selected body or branch, and induct on its length.
This gives the same additive cost equations. Choices belong to individual
call occurrences, and the language has no shared mutable state, so serializing
the argument trees introduces no constraint on their choices. No successful
tree uses a zero divisor or terminates through $\operatorname{loop}$.
The exact-cost equivalence is
\lean{RegisterProgram.Syntax.Machine.eval_iff_steps}, and transition
locality is \lean{RegisterProgram.Syntax.Machine.Step.context}. The
machine versions of correctness and convergence follow, and
\lean{RegisterProgram.Syntax.Machine.arbitrarily_small_program}
states the bound directly for finite transition sequences.
This formalizes the machine needed here, not the book's theory for arbitrary
structures and program vocabularies.

\subsection{Integer parameters used in Lean}
To avoid real rounding in the construction itself, the formal proof uses, for
$j=\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor\geq32$,
\[
p=\lfloor j/4\rfloor,\quad k=2^p,\quad h=2^{2p}+1,\quad
J=\lfloor n/p\rfloor+1,\quad
\ell_1=\lceil3n/4\rceil,\quad \ell_2=\lceil n/3\rceil.
\]
Write $\widehat T(X)=\max_{a,b\leq X}G(a,b)$. The finite construction proves,
for arbitrary natural parameters satisfying $k\geq1$, $k^2<h$, $Y\geq1$,
$2X\leq ZY$, and $X\leq Zk^J$,
\[
\widehat T(X)\leq\max\{h+1+\widehat T(Y),\ h+3J+2+2\widehat T(Z)\}.
\]
Taking $X=2^n$, $Y=2^{\ell_1}$, $Z=2^{\ell_2}$ (so $Y$ is the early
threshold and $Z$ the target modulus) gives the required hypotheses.
Lean checks
\[
(h+1)j\leq64n,\quad (h+3J+2)j\leq64n,\quad
j-2\leq\log_2^{\mathrm{nat}}\ell_1,\log_2^{\mathrm{nat}}\ell_2,
\quad 8\ell_1\leq7n,\quad16\ell_2\leq7n.
\]
The last estimates and $8j\leq9(j-2)$ give a factor $63/64$ for either
recursive contribution after clearing denominators. Taking $C\geq4096$ and
large enough for the finite initial segment proves
$\widehat T(2^n)\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor\leq Cn$ for all $n$.
Comparing integer floors and ceilings with real base-two logarithms yields the
stated real bound. These parameter choices change only absolute constants,
not the proof mechanism or its asymptotic order. The refinement in
\lean{ShortGCD.SharpBound} uses the tunable parameters of
Corollary~\ref{cor:six}, with recursive size bounds
$2^{\lceil\alpha n\rceil}$ and $2^{\lceil\beta n\rceil}$. It applies the same
finite recurrence, uses the original bound to justify a finite upper limit,
and checks the rounding limits needed to preserve the coefficient $6$.
